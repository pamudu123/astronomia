# Sinhala and Tamil New Year (Aluth Avurudda / Puthandu): background reference

Background for the site assistant, researched November 2026.

- Everything about auspicious times describes **tradition and belief**, not proven fact.
- No nakath times for any particular year are included on purpose. Those come from the site's own calculation.
- "(unverified)" marks points that could not be confirmed in a reliable source.

---

## 1. When and why it happens

**The astrological moment**

- The Sinhala New Year, අලුත් අවුරුද්ද (Aluth Avurudda), and the Tamil New Year, புத்தாண்டு (Puthandu), are celebrated at the same moment. It is officially called the Sinhala and Tamil New Year, or the Sinhala and Hindu New Year.
- In traditional astrology, the new year begins when the Sun leaves මීන රාශිය (Meena rashiya, Pisces) and enters මේෂ රාශිය (Mesha rashiya, Aries). Mesha is the first sign of the zodiac, so this moment ends one solar year and starts the next.
- The Sun's move from one sign to the next is called සංක්‍රාන්තිය (sankranthiya).
- Sri Lankan and Indian traditional calendars measure the zodiac against the stars. This is the sidereal zodiac. Western astrology uses the tropical zodiac, which follows the seasons. The two have slowly drifted apart because the Earth's axis wobbles (precession). That is why the Sun enters sidereal Mesha in mid April, not around 21 March.
- The transition currently falls on **13 or 14 April**. Some sources give a wider range of 12 to 15 April. The exact moment changes every year and can come at any hour of the day or night.
- Tradition also links the season to the Sun passing directly over Sri Lanka. Some astrologers believe Aries sits right above the island, though they disagree on the exact spot.

**Bak and the harvest**

- April is බක් (Bak) in the Sinhala calendar. The name is usually traced to the Sanskrit word *bhagya*, meaning fortune or plenty.
- The main harvest of the මහ කන්නය (Maha kannaya, the main rice season) ends around March or is gathered in Bak. Sources put this slightly differently.
- With full granaries and little field work before the next season (යල, Yala), villagers had food and time to celebrate. The festival is often described as a thanksgiving.
- Farmers traditionally offer the first of the new rice to temples, often as milk rice.

## 2. The neutral period: nonagathaya or punya kalaya

**What it is**

- නොනගතය (nonagathaya) is the gap between the old year and the new. It belongs to neither year, so it is treated as neutral. Official English tables often call it an "inauspicious time".
- It is also called පුණ්‍ය කාලය (punya kalaya), "time for merit", because it is meant for religious activity.
- A popular explanation says the name means "no nakath" (no auspicious times). At least one Sri Lankan astrologer calls this folk etymology and links the word to the Sun touching, then fully entering, Aries. The word's origin is uncertain.

**How its length is worked out**

- Tradition gives the neutral period a fixed length of **12 hours 48 minutes**.
- This is explained as the time the Sun takes to cross fully from Pisces into Aries. It starts when the Sun's disc first touches Aries and ends when the whole disc has entered.
- The New Year moment (the "dawn") is the **midpoint**. The period therefore starts 6 hours 24 minutes before the dawn and ends 6 hours 24 minutes after it. The official tables for 2024 and 2026 both follow this exactly.
- Some writers say the first half is only for religious practice. The household rituals, such as lighting the hearth, fall in the second half. In 2024 the state committee said these rituals had to fit within the 6 hours 24 minutes after the transition. They also had to avoid රාහු කාලය (Rahu kalaya), a daily period traditionally considered unlucky.
- For comparison, the Sun's disc is about half a degree wide and the Sun moves about one degree a day. Crossing a boundary takes roughly half a day, which is close to the traditional figure.
- 12 hours 48 minutes equals 32 units of 24 minutes, a traditional South Asian time unit (ghatika). No source says the figure was derived this way (unverified).

**What people do and avoid**

- Old-year tasks are finished before the period starts, and nothing new is begun.
- Work, business and normal activity stop across the country.
- Many households do not cook or eat. They wait for the auspicious time to light the hearth.
- People go to the temple (Buddhists) or kovil (Hindus) to worship and do meritorious deeds.
- One encyclopaedia entry says traditional games are also acceptable.
- How strictly families keep these customs varies. The Tamil tradition has no equal period of stopping everything (see section 6).

## 3. The New Year nakath, in order

නැකත් (nakath, also spelled nekath) are auspicious times. Each year's list is published as a නැකත් සීට්ටුව (nakath seettuwa, auspicious-times sheet).

For most rituals the sheet gives a **time**, a **direction** to face (දිශාව, disawa) and a **colour** to wear. These change from ritual to ritual and every year. Some entries also name herbs, leaves or foods.

The usual order is below. Small differences between years are normal.

1. **Viewing the moon** (some sheets only). Sources describe this either as viewing the old year's moon or the new crescent.

2. **Bathing for the old year**: පරණ අවුරුද්ද සඳහා ස්නානය (parana avurudda sandaha snanaya). On the last day of the old year, people wash with නානු (nanu), a herbal mixture. The sheet usually gives a date rather than an exact minute, and sometimes names a herb to add.

3. **Dawn of the New Year**: අලුත් අවුරුදු උදාව (aluth avurudu udava). The moment the Sun is reckoned to enter Mesha. It is greeted with firecrackers, temple bells and the රබාන (rabana, a large drum).

4. **The neutral period** (see section 2). The sheet gives start and end times, with the dawn in the middle.

5. **Lighting the hearth and cooking**: ලිප ගිනි මෙලවීම (lipa gini meleveema), also listed as ආහාර පිසීම (ahara piseema).
   - Usually the woman of the house lights a fresh fire at the set time, facing the set direction and wearing the set colour.
   - Milk is boiled in a new clay pot and allowed to overflow, a sign of plenty.
   - Then කිරිබත් (kiribath, milk rice) is cooked.

6. **Starting work, first transactions and the first meal**: වැඩ ඇල්ලීම, ගනුදෙනු කිරීම හා ආහාර අනුභවය (weda alleema, ganu denu kireema ha ahara anubhavaya). One time covers three acts.
   - *Starting work*: children open their books. Adults do a token piece of their usual work.
   - *ගනුදෙනු (ganu denu, "give and take")*: the year's first exchange of money. Younger people offer බුලත් (bulath, betel leaves) to elders and receive blessings and money. In some villages a coin is dropped into the well and water is drawn into a new pot.
   - *First meal*: the family eats milk rice together, often with sweets and ලුණු මිරිස් (lunu miris, an onion and chilli relish).

7. **Anointing oil on the head**: හිස තෙල් ගෑම (hisa thel gaema). This is usually a day or more after the dawn.
   - An elder, or the temple monk, applies herbal oil to each person's head.
   - Leaves are placed above the head and under the feet, for example කොහොඹ (kohomba, neem) above and කොළොන් (kolon) below. The leaves change with the year and the weekday.
   - The person then bathes facing the auspicious direction. It is believed to bring good health.
   - A state ceremony is held each year at the Natha Devalaya, in the Temple of the Tooth premises in Kandy.

8. **Leaving home for work**: රැකී රක්ෂා සඳහා පිටත්ව යාම (rakee raksha sandaha pitathva yaama). The first trip out to work after the holiday. Some sheets say what to eat before leaving.

9. **Planting a tree**: පැළ සිටුවීම (pela situveema). This has appeared in the official list in recent years.

The list is not fixed. In 2026, for example, extra items were added. The assistant should rely on the site's own nakath for the current year.

## 4. Who publishes the official nakath

**How it started**

- Traditionally, the village temple's chief monk or a local astrologer worked out the times.
- Because times differed from village to village, W. J. M. Lokubandara, as Minister of Cultural Affairs, set up a state committee so the whole country could follow the same moments.

**The state committee**

- For many years the State Astrologers' Committee, under the Department of Cultural Affairs (සංස්කෘතික කටයුතු දෙපාර්තමේන්තුව), prepared the official sheet. Each year it was formally presented to the President.
- In 2024 the committee had 42 members. They included the authors of six almanacs (ලිත්, lith) and heads of traditional astrology lineages.

**Recent changes**

- Astrologers do not always agree. In 2024 the transition fell at night. Some astrologers wanted the rituals moved to the next morning, but the committee kept the night-time times.
- The committee was later dissolved. Reports say this happened in 2024.
- In March 2026, after a public dispute, the Ministry of Buddhasasana, Religious and Cultural Affairs released a single table, agreed with almanac authors and other astrologers, as the official state table.
- The Ministry issued the 2027 table in August 2026. These arrangements may change again.

**Other sources of times**

- Many families follow a printed almanac or their own family astrologer.
- Hindu Tamil families generally follow their panchangam (almanac) and temple priests.
- Visitors may therefore see different times for the same year. Within the tradition these reflect different calculation choices, not simple errors.

## 5. Customs

**Before the day**

- Families clean the house and garden, whitewash or paint, and throw out old things.
- People buy new clothes, often in the year's lucky colour, and make sweets in advance.

**Signs of the season**

- කොහා (koha), the Asian koel, is traditionally the herald of the New Year. Its loud call starts around March.
- Biologically, the call is the male's breeding call. Koels lay their eggs in crows' nests and live in Sri Lanka all year; they are not migrants.
- The red flowers of the එරබදු (erabadu, coral tree) bloom in early April and are also taken as a sign.

**Food**

- කිරිබත් (kiribath, milk rice) is the centre of the table.
- Sweets include කැවුම් (kavum, oil cakes), කොකිස් (kokis, a crisp fried sweet said to be of Dutch origin), ආස්මී (aasmi), අලුවා (aluwa), අග්ගලා (aggala) and තලගුලි (thalaguli, sesame balls).
- Neighbours swap plates of sweets. By custom, a plate is not returned empty.

**Visits and gifts**

- Families visit relatives and friends, sometimes for about a week, and patch up quarrels.
- Visitors bring sweets, betel leaves or gifts such as clothes. Arriving empty-handed is considered improper.
- Younger people offer betel leaves to elders, who bless them.

**Games**

- කොට්ට පොර (kotta pora): two players on a raised pole try to knock each other off with pillows.
- ඔළිඳ කෙළිය (olinda keliya): a seed-and-pits board game related to mancala, often played by women.
- පංච කෙළිය (pancha keliya): a board game where thrown shells decide each move.
- ඔංචිලි පැදීම (onchili padeema): swinging, with traditional songs.
- කණා මුට්ටි බිඳීම (kana mutti bindeema): a blindfolded player tries to break a clay pot.
- ලිස්සන ගහ නැගීම (lissana gaha nageema): climbing a greased pole for a prize.
- කඹ ඇදීම (kamba adeema): tug-of-war.
- අලියාට ඇස තැබීම (aliyata asa thabeema): a blindfolded player places the eye on a drawn elephant.
- Communities also hold festivals with drumming and අවුරුදු කුමරිය (Avurudu Kumari, New Year princess) contests.

## 6. Tamil Puthandu: what is different

**Shared and different**

- Puthandu marks the same moment. It is the first day of சித்திரை (Chithirai), the first month of the Tamil solar calendar.
- A Hindu affairs official at the Ministry has said Tamil Hindu practice has no nonagathaya-style pause. He also said the milk-boiling and first-meal nakath are not central to it. This is a single source. Another source mentions Tamil families visiting temples during the "punya kaalam", so practice varies.

**The main rites**

- *Herbal bath*: மருத்து நீர் (maruthu neer) is herbal water made by temple priests from herbs, flowers, milk and saffron. It is applied to each person's head before bathing, at a time given by the priest or almanac.
- *New clothes*: worn in colours set by the பஞ்சாங்கம் (panchangam, Tamil almanac).
- *Home*: the house is cleaned, a mango-leaf garland is hung over the door, and கோலம் (kolam) patterns are drawn in rice flour at the entrance.
- *Puja*: a lamp is lit beside a pot of water topped with mango leaves and a coconut. The Sun god (Surya) and Ganesha are worshipped.
- *Pongal*: milk is boiled in a new pot until it overflows, and sweet rice (pongal) is made.
- *Temple*: families visit the kovil with offerings.
- *First money*: in கைவிசேஷம் (kai vishesham), elders give money to younger family members, sometimes wrapped in betel leaves.
- *Food*: mango pachadi mixes sweet, sour and bitter tastes to stand for life's mixed experiences.
- *Games*: these include போர்த்தேங்காய் (por thenkai, a coconut-smashing contest) in the north and east, and pot-breaking.
- *Later business nakath*: one source mentions a business-opening nakath some weeks later, linked to the goddess Lakshmi (unverified).
