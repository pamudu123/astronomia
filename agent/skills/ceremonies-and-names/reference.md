# Life ceremonies in Sri Lanka where astrology is used

Background notes for the site's chat assistant. Researched November 2026. Everything here describes tradition and belief. It does not claim that astrology works.

## 0. Framing notes for the assistant

- **One word, two meanings.** In Sinhala, නැකත (nakatha) can mean a birth star (one of 27 lunar mansions) or an auspicious moment chosen by an astrologer. The plural නැකත් (nakath) usually means auspicious times. Make clear which meaning you are using.
- **Who uses it.** Astrology matters most to many Buddhist and Hindu Sri Lankans. Families consult an astrologer (ජ්‍යොතිෂවේදියා, jyotishavediya) about marriage, naming, first solid food, first letters and ear piercing.
- **Practice varies** by family, region, caste, religion and astrologer. Many families follow only some customs, or none.
- **Astrologers disagree.** In 2024 the state committee and a rival group published different New Year times.
- **Not everyone accepts it.** Critics in the Sri Lankan press call astrology a pseudoscience. Buddhist scripture also warns against waiting for lucky stars, for example in the Nakkhatta Jataka, where a man misses his own wedding this way.

## 1. Naming a baby by birth star

**The basic idea**
- Astrologers divide the Moon's path into 27 nakath. Each one covers 13°20′ and is split into four quarters, called පාදය (pādaya, "pada"), of 3°20′ each. That makes 108 quarters in all.
- In the widely used Indian-derived table, each quarter has a traditional starting syllable for a child's name. For example, the four quarters of Ashwini (අස්විද, aswida) are given as chu, che, cho and la. **The site uses its own Sinhala letter table**: always give the letters from the page or the tools, not from this example.
- The baby's quarter depends on where the Moon was at the moment of birth. This is why the exact birth time matters.
- Some families call the suggested letters නම් අකුරු (nam akuru, "name letters"). This exact term did not appear in any source (unverified as a fixed term).

**How Sri Lankan practice differs**
- Not every Sri Lankan astrologer uses the quarter table. One Sri Lankan guide describes three methods: letters suited to the ලග්නය (lagnaya, rising sign), letters from the birth nakatha, and letters from the weekday of birth. It ranks the rising-sign method highest.
- Some Sinhala astrologers also use syllable groupings (gana) and numerology. Hindu families may use letters tied to the birth sign.
- The astrologer usually suggests a few letters. Parents or elders then pick a name that starts with one of them.

**Why families do it**
- Many believe a name starting with a well-matched sound supports the child's health, long life and good fortune. Others follow it as family custom or out of respect for elders.
- Many modern parents treat it as optional, or check a freely chosen name with an astrologer. Muslim families usually take names from religious sources or relatives' and the imam's suggestions, not astrology.

**The naming occasion**
- The Sinhala naming ceremony, නම් තැබීම (nam tæbīma), is often held at an auspicious time.
- Older sources link it to the baby's first rice meal, giving a "rice name" (bat nama). Timing varied: the day of birth, the fifth day, or the seventh month.

**Accuracy note:** The Moon moves about 13° a day, so a birth time that is off by a few hours can change the quarter and the suggested letter.

## 2. The first letters ceremony (අකුරු කියවීම, akuru kiyaweema)

**What it is**
- A ceremony that marks a child's first reading and writing of letters.
- It is now mostly symbolic. Children used to learn to write in a sand tray, වැලි පිල්ල (væli pilla). Today the sand tray survives mainly as part of this ceremony.

**When it is done**
- Usually between about 2 and 5 years old. A University of Colombo resource says it is traditionally held early, at around 2 to 3.
- A village study in Ranamure says it is held at age three, if the child already shows an interest in words.
- The day and time come from an astrologer's nakath, based on the child's horoscope or birth details. In Ranamure, families might instead seek the auspicious sign from an uncle or the temple's senior monk.

**Who leads it**
- Traditionally a Buddhist monk led it, often at the temple. Today it may be a parent, a grandparent, an uncle, a respected teacher or a monk. In Ranamure, the child's uncle often leads it.
- Sinhala diaspora schools abroad also hold group ceremonies.

**What usually happens** (details vary)
1. A table is set with a Buddha image. Many families add pictures of Saraswati and Ganesh, Hindu deities linked to learning. Oil lamps are lit before the auspicious time.
2. At the nakath, the elder reads the first letters aloud and the child repeats them.
3. The elder guides the child's hand to write a few letters, traditionally in sand or on a slate, ගල් ලෑල්ල (gal lælla). Today paper or a tray of rice is often used.
4. Sources disagree on the first letter. Some say it is usually ශ්‍රී (shri) or the first vowel අ (a), but practice varies.
5. The child offers betel leaves, බුලත් (bulath), to the teacher and elders and worships them. Pirith chanting may follow.

**History:** A 1902 account describes a similar rite: the boy made offerings to Ganesh, washed his teacher's feet and offered betel before receiving his book.

**Not confirmed:** No reliable source says it is normally held in the morning or that the child faces a set direction (both unverified). Astrologers often give a direction and colour for other events, so some families may receive these too.

## 3. Weddings

### 3a. Horoscope matching: porondam (පොරොන්දම්)
- Before a proposal goes far, often before the engagement, the families exchange horoscopes, කේන්දර (kendara). An astrologer compares them.
- Sinhala matching is described as a twenty-point check, විසි පොරොන්දම් (visi porondam). Tamil families use a similar system called porutham, usually with ten points.
- Astrologers also look for chart "blemishes" (dosha). The best known is කුජ දෝෂය (kuja dōshaya), a placement of Mars. Newspaper marriage ads often mention kuja, either asking for a matching chart or saying it does not matter.
- Even writers within astrology say the porondam score should not be the final word. Some families see a poor match as a reason to stop; others ignore it.

### 3b. Choosing the wedding nakath
- After matching, an astrologer chooses the date and times from both horoscopes. Sources differ on whether the bride's or the groom's family arranges this; it varies.
- The astrologer may also allow for the venue, travel time between the two homes and dressing time.
- The times go on a nakath sheet, නැකත් පත්‍රය (nakath pathraya). It can cover the groom leaving home, stepping onto and off the poruwa, leaving the venue, entering the groom's home and the homecoming.
- **Direction:** The astrologer names the direction the poruwa should face and the direction the couple face when they step onto it.
- **Colours:** Nakath sheets for the New Year routinely list colours to wear. For weddings, a colour for the bride or groom is commonly said to be included, but this could not be confirmed (unverified).
- **Avoided days:** One veteran ashtaka reciter says weddings are not held on Tuesdays, which are seen as inauspicious. Treat this as a common belief, not a rule.

### 3c. The poruwa ceremony (පෝරුව චාරිත්‍රය, poruwa chārithraya)
The poruwa is a decorated platform at the centre of a Sinhala Buddhist wedding. It is covered with white cloth and strewn with rice or paddy, coins and betel. Four clay pots, පුන් කලස (pun kalasa), holding coconut flowers stand at the corners, and oil lamps are lit. Sources order the steps slightly differently. A typical sequence:

1. **Arrival:** The bride's younger brother washes the groom's feet or pours water over his shoes. The groom gives him a ring or gift.
2. **Ascending:** At the nakath, the couple are led onto the poruwa, often by their maternal uncles (මාමා, māmā), and step on right foot first.
3. **Betel:** The couple exchange or drop seven sheaves of betel. This is said to honour seven generations of relatives.
4. **Ashtaka:** Between the rituals, an elder recites blessing verses in Pali and Sanskrit, called අෂ්ටක (ashtaka).
5. **Gifts:** The groom places a necklace on the bride and may give her clothes, a sign that he will provide for her. Exchanging rings is a newer addition.
6. **Tying and water:** An uncle ties the couple's little fingers, or thumbs, with thread. Water, often blessed by pirith chanting, is poured over their joined hands from a spouted jug (kendiya).
7. **Song:** Girls sing the ජයමංගල ගාථා (jayamangala gāthā), Buddhist verses of victory and blessing.
8. **Thanks to the mother:** The groom gives the bride's mother a long white cloth (redi kachchiya) to thank her for raising her daughter.
9. **Feeding and respect:** The couple feed each other කිරිබත් (kiribath, milk rice) and may share milk. They offer betel to their parents and elders.
10. **Descending:** At another nakath, the couple step down and a coconut is split. A clean split is read as a good sign.

The ceremony once lasted about 90 minutes. Today it is often around 30 minutes, mostly because fewer verses are recited.

### 3d. Homecoming (ගෙදර යාම, gedara yāma)
- People also say ගෙදර එනවා (gedara enawa). It marks the bride's arrival at the groom's family home.
- In the past, the groom took the bride home in a procession on the wedding day itself, with her family going along. Today it is usually a separate party a few days later, hosted by the groom's family.
- The astrologer may set the time for leaving the wedding venue and for entering the groom's house.
- Some guides say the bride is welcomed with milk or lights a lamp in her new home (unverified; informal sources only).

### 3e. Kandyan and low-country customs (brief)
- **Shared rituals:** Wikipedia says today's poruwa ceremony mixes up-country (Kandyan) and low-country customs. The core rituals are common across Sinhala areas.
- **Kandyan dress:** The Kandyan bride wears the ඔසරිය (osariya), a sari draped with a frill at the waist, along with traditional jewellery and headpiece. The groom may wear the Nilame costume.
- **Low-country differences:** No well-sourced detail was found (not covered).
- **Kandyan law:** Kandyan customary law recognises දීග (dīga) marriage, where the bride moves to the husband's home, and බින්න (binna) marriage, where the husband moves into the bride's family home. The type of marriage affects inheritance.

## 4. Other occasions where nakath are commonly used

- **House warming (ගෘහ ප්‍රවේශය, gruha praveshaya):** Astrologers commonly give a nakath for first entering a new house. Buddhist families often hold pirith chanting and a දානය (dānaya, almsgiving for monks). Boiling milk until it overflows, කිරි ඉතිරවීම (kiri ithirawīma), is well documented for the New Year, but for house warmings only in informal sources (unverified).
- **Laying foundation stones (මුල්ගල් තැබීම, mulgal thæbīma):** Typically done at an auspicious time after pirith, often with monks present, even for company and military buildings. The stone may be hollow and filled with auspicious metals and spices.
- **First rice feeding (ඉඳුල් කට ගෑම, indul kata gæma, also බත් කැවීම, bath kævīma):** Traditionally between 5 and 12 months, with the father or paternal grandfather giving the first mouthful. An astrologer picks the day and hour. A 1902 account says food and coins were laid on a plantain leaf, and the first thing the baby touched was read as a sign.
- **First visit or outing of a newborn:** An old custom, දොරට වැඩීම (dorata vædīma), took the baby outside to see the morning sun a few days after birth. No reliable source was found on nakath for a baby's first trip home or first visits to relatives or temple (unverified). Ear piercing (කන් විදීම, kan vidīma) and the first haircut are also traditionally timed.
- **Starting a business:** Many people open a shop, sign contracts or begin a venture at a nakath. The press describes this, but there is no survey of how common it is. At New Year, the first money exchange (ගනු දෙනු, ganu denu) has its own time.
- **Travel and starting work:** Nakath can cover setting out on a journey or starting a job, often with a direction and colour. The 2026 New Year sheet, for example, did this for leaving for urgent duties and returning to work.

## 5. Old horoscopes (කේන්දර, kendara)

**Keeping them**
- Traditionally an astrologer draws up a child's කේන්දරය (kendaraya, birth chart) soon after birth. The family keeps it for life and consults it for naming, milestones, marriage matching and hard times.
- Older charts were written with a stylus on palm leaves, පුස්කොළ (puskola, "ola"); later ones are on paper.
- The British Library has digitised family palm-leaf birth charts from Jaffna and Batticaloa, one dated 1955. They record planetary positions and period readings.
- Many such records are lost. Northern Tamil families lost horoscopes in the war, and palm leaves are easily damaged by mould.

**Why recover a birth date from an old chart**
- **Official papers:**
  - The Registrar General's Department accepts a horoscope prepared close to the birth as a supporting document for late birth registration, when hospital or village-officer records are missing.
  - A 2026 circular from the Department for Registration of Persons lets citizens over 50 without a birth certificate apply for a National Identity Card. It lists a certified photocopy of the traditional horoscope (Kendaraya) made at birth as acceptable proof.
- **Missing or unclear dates:** An old chart may be faded or use traditional reckoning, yet still show where the planets were. Saturn takes about 29.5 years and Jupiter about 12 to circle the Sun. Combined with the Sun's sign and the Moon's nakatha, this can narrow down the real birth date. Which calendar a given old chart uses is unverified.
- **Family history and new readings:** People want an accurate date to cast a modern chart, run porondam for a proposal, or record an ancestor's birthday.
